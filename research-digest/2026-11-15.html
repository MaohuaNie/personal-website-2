1Uncertainty Attitudes, Subjective Beliefs, and Decisions Under Uncertainty

Delavande, A., Ganguli, J., Mengel, F.

This paper uses survey experiments to jointly elicit uncertainty attitudes (risk, ambiguity, probability weighting, loss aversion) and subjective beliefs in health and financial contexts, then estimates structural models to compare prospect theory with subjective expected utility across a general population sample. It finds high heterogeneity in both preferences and beliefs, and that prospect theory with source-dependent weighting outperforms SEU particularly when beliefs are concentrated at extreme values.

2026-11Relevance 0.66doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06232

AbstractWe conducted survey experiments to gather new data on uncertainty attitudes and subjective beliefs. Our survey targets a general population, focusing on health and financial decisions. We collect data on (i) individual attitudes toward risk and ambiguity, including individual-specific probability weighting and loss aversion, and (ii) individual probabilistic beliefs about events related to the decisions we consider. The joint availability of individual preference parameters and subjective beliefs allows us to study heterogeneity and cross-correlations across these parameters in depth and to estimate structural models of decision making under uncertainty that go beyond subjective expected utility. We find that both preferences and beliefs are highly heterogeneous. Compared with subjective expected utility, prospect theory combined with source-dependent probability weighting fits the data strictly better in some contexts and equally well in others. Prospect theory performs particularly well when the belief distribution is concentrated toward extreme values. 2Asset Pricing in a World of Imperfect Foresight

Bossaerts, P., Fattinger, F., van den Bogaerde, F., Yang, W.

The paper proposes 'dynamic narrow framing' as a behavioral explanation for why asset prices can still reflect perfect-foresight equilibria even when agents have imperfect foresight, verifying this with a controlled experiment that evaluates both prices and individual choices.

2026-11Relevance 0.51doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2025.03690

AbstractA key assumption of dynamic asset pricing theory is that agents have perfect foresight: for all future contingencies, they correctly foresee the corresponding equilibrium prices. Is it possible for prices to still reflect perfect foresight even if agents have imperfect foresight? We answer affirmatively, provided agents exhibit a mild form of narrow framing, which we refer to as dynamic narrow framing: while accounting for future endowments, agents ignore retrading opportunities. This behavior vastly simplifies computation of optimal choices because it obviates the need to form beliefs about future prices. Obviously, choices will generally be different, and hence, suboptimal, compared with those that obtain if agents were to optimize dynamically using perfect foresight about future prices. With a controlled experiment, we verify that our behavioral assumption explains both prices and choices. Our findings allow us to reinterpret the successes (when evaluating prices only) and failures (when evaluating prices against choices) of traditional tests of asset pricing theory on historical data from the field. This paper was accepted by Lukas Schmid, finance. Funding: P. 3Learning and probability matching in newsvendor decisions

Han, W., Wang, X.

This paper examines why human decisions in the newsvendor paradigm deviate from optimal profit maximization, finding via behavioral experiments and EEG that repeated feedback induces probability matching and asymmetric belief updating rather than utility distortion, and develops a formal learning model that better fits observed choices.

2026-11Relevance 0.61doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2026.107776

AbstractHuman decision making in the newsvendor paradigm systematically deviates from the predictions of expected profit maximization, yet the cognitive mechanisms underlying these deviations remain contested. We investigate whether these biases arise from distorted utility evaluation or from adaptive learning induced by repeated feedback. Across three laboratory experiments ( N = 214 ), we compare behavior under decision from description, decision from experience, and newsvendor environments while varying information availability. The first experiment combines behavioral and electroencephalographic (EEG) data, the second replicates the behavioral findings without EEG, and the third extends the analysis to a multi-alternative setting with continuous demand. We document two robust patterns in newsvendor environments: decision accuracy inferior to random guessing, and positive correlation between choice and realized demand. These patterns are absent in one-shot descriptive decisions and cannot be explained by standard utility-based models. Neural evidence shows that repeated environments engage parietal and frontal activity associated with choice deliberation and feedback-based learning, and that memory encoding differs systematically across demand outcomes. Motivated by these findings, we develop a model incorporating probability matching and asymmetric belief updating, which provides a substantially improved fit to observed choices. Our results demonstrate that repeated feedback fundamentally alters the mechanism of economic decision making, highlighting the central role of learning and belief dynamics in generating newsvendor biases.

4Channeled Attention and Stable Errors

Gagnon-Bartsch, T., Rabin, M., Schwartzstein, J.

The paper presents a theoretical model in which people filter incoming data through their existing (potentially misspecified) model, causing certain errors to be 'attentionally stable' and persist indefinitely even with abundant feedback; it uses this framework to explain asymmetries in error detection, coarse categorization, and why outsiders spot mistakes that insiders miss.

2026-11Relevance 0.5doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjag055

AbstractAbstract People persist in mistaken beliefs even in settings with high stakes, abundant data, and repeated opportunities to learn—settings where standard arguments imply they should correct their errors. We develop an attention-based framework for understanding why errors persist. When a person channels his attention through the lens of his potentially misspecified model, exposure to data is not the same as tracking the statistics necessary to tell him his model is wrong. A model is attentionally stable when the data the person deems relevant will, in the long run, fail to make the model look implausible. Models that erroneously rule things out—e.g., beliefs that a variable doesn’t matter or that a distinction doesn’t exist—tend to be attentionally stable because they divert attention away from data that would refute the models. Conversely, models that erroneously rule things in are less likely to be stable. Whether an error is discovered depends on the structure of the decision problem but is not tightly linked to its level of harm; arbitrarily costly errors can persist while trivially inexpensive ones may be detected. Nevertheless, no error is robust both to changes in the decision environment and to shocks that expand attention: every channeled-attention error is vulnerable to some perturbation that would reveal it. We use the framework to analyze the stability of psychological biases and empirical misconceptions, as well as to explain why fresh eyes catch mistakes, why people use overly coarse rather than overly fine categorizations, and why people recognize errors in others that they miss in themselves.

JEP: General

7Adaptive regulation of confidence following metacognitive conflict: Evidence from behavior and computational modeling.

Xing, J., Herregods, S., Vermeylen, L., Desender, K.

This study investigates how conflicts between internal confidence signals and external feedback ('metacognitive conflict') affect subsequent confidence reporting in perceptual decision making, using behavioral experiments and an evidence accumulation model with post-decisional accumulation to explain the underlying mechanisms.

2026-11Relevance 0.51doi.org/10.1037/xge0001997

AbstractIt is well established that external feedback is effective not only for enhancing cognitive performance but also for regulating metacognitive monitoring. However, external feedback could be detrimental when it conflicts with one's internal feedback signal (i.e., the level of confidence we have in our decisions). Currently, however, it is unknown how discrepancies between internal and external feedback ("metacognitive conflict") influence cognitive and metacognitive performance. Here, we examined this question in a series of perceptual decision-making experiments in which participants reported both their level of confidence and received performance feedback. Metacognitive conflict occurs if participants have high confidence in their decision, but external feedback indicates that the decision was erroneous, or vice versa. Results revealed that metacognitive conflict triggers next-trial behavioral adjustments: Participants exhibit significantly lower confidence following metacognitive conflict. Furthermore, this behavioral adjustment was smaller in blocks with a high versus low proportion of metacognitive conflict trials, and confidence was generally reduced in blocks with a high proportion of metacognitive conflict. To better characterize the computational mechanisms underlying this effect, we deployed an evidence accumulation model with additional postdecision accumulation to quantify confidence. Model fits revealed that participants become more conservative in reporting confidence after experiencing metacognitive conflict, as evidenced by biased postdecision starting points. Jointly, our findings provide much-needed insight into the metacognitive consequences of metacognitive conflict. (PsycInfo Database Record (c) 2026 APA, all rights reserved).

8Time to jump: Exploring the distribution of noise in evidence accumulation as a function of time pressure

Liss, J., von Krause, M., Elsemüller, L., Hunsmann, E., Lerche, V.

The paper extends the diffusion model of binary decision making by investigating a Lévy-flight model that allows heavy-tailed noise in evidence accumulation, testing how time pressure systematically shifts the stability parameter governing jump size across two discrimination tasks with ~500 participants. Using simulation-based Bayesian inference, it shows that stricter deadlines produce more heavy-tailed, near-symmetric RT distributions and that the Lévy-flight model outperforms standard diffusion models even without time pressure.

2026-11Relevance 0.5doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2026.101836

AbstractThe diffusion model conceptualizes binary decisions as emerging from continuous evidence accumulation with Gaussian noise. The Lévy-flight model (LFM) extends this by allowing accumulation noise to follow a heavier-tailed distribution, enabling sudden, large jumps in evidence accumulation. The heavy-tailedness is governed by the stability parameter α, with smaller α-values indicating more frequent and larger jumps. We demonstrate through simulation that the LFM can generate near-symmetrical response time (RT) distributions typical under strict deadlines. In Study 1, we explored the influence of intense time pressure on α in a letter-number discrimination task, expecting higher time pressure to entail smaller α-values. Participants (N = 341) were assigned to an accuracy (unlimited RT), implicit deadline (feedback discouraging RTs over 500 ms), or explicit deadline condition (timeout at 500 ms). Given the LFM's intractable likelihood, we used BayesFlow, a framework for simulation-based inference with deep learning. Model comparisons consistently favored the LFM over diffusion model variants-even in the accuracy condition. Importantly, α-values decreased with increasing time pressure and were especially low in the explicit deadline condition, where RT distributions also approached symmetry. Estimating both the LFM and a structurally matched diffusion model, with and without timeout modeling, further showed that accounting for timeout-related RT truncation led to substantially lower diffusion model drift rates, whereas LFM drift rates changed only modestly. Study 2 (N = 160) replicated α's sensitivity to time pressure in a brightness discrimination task. Overall, α responded systematically to temporal constraints across both tasks and timeout modeling specifications.

10The decision to stop “counting” explains numerical underproduction in primates

Seidler, L., Jannasch, L., Westendorff, S., Nieder, A.

Rhesus macaques trained to produce target numerosities show systematic underproduction and asymmetric reaction times; a stochastic accumulation-to-bound model accounts for both patterns, framing numerical stopping as a sequential decision process.

2026-11Relevance 0.38doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2620933123

AbstractSequential numerical production requires generating a target number of actions, but the mechanisms governing the termination of counting sequences are unclear. We trained two rhesus macaques to produce instructed numerosities by generating the corresponding number of actions in response to sign numerals or dot arrays. Although action sequences were controlled for temporal parameters, both monkeys performed accurately across both stimulus formats. However, their performance accuracy showed a systematic asymmetry: underproduction occurred more often than overproduction, contrary to predictions from perceptual numerosity judgments governed by Weber–Fechner scaling. This asymmetry was mirrored in reaction times, with longer latencies for underproduced sequences and shorter latencies for overproduced ones. A stochastic accumulation-to-bound model in which a decision variable evolves across counting steps and triggers termination when a threshold is reached can account for both accuracy and reaction-time patterns. These results provide evidence for a sequential stopping process that contributes to numerical production, relating counting behavior to general principles of decision formation.

11The Gambler’s Fallacy Fallacy Fallacy (Commentary on Xiang et al., 2025)

Banki, D., Simonsohn, U.

This commentary reanalyzes data from Xiang et al. (2025) and argues, contrary to their conclusion, that the gambler's fallacy does originate in biased probability beliefs, presenting new evidence that at least 80% of gambler's-fallacy predictions stem from such biases.

2026-11Relevance 0.33doi.org/10.1177/09567976261490829

AbstractXiang et al. concluded that the gambler’s fallacy does not originate in biased beliefs. Reanalyzing their data, we arrived at the opposite conclusion. We show, more generally, that their main analysis—examining whether the median belief is biased—is not diagnostic of the presence of the gambler’s fallacy, whereas our test of the share of probability estimates for the streak ending above or below 50% is diagnostic. We also report results from a new study in which at least 80% of gambler’s-fallacy predictions originated in biased beliefs.

12Anterior cingulate neurons perform parallel processing of fixated and peripheral information to guide information sampling and choice

Butler, J., Muller, T., Veselic, S., Malalasekera, W., Behrens, T., Hunt, L., Kennerley, S.

Using rhesus macaques in a multi-cue decision task, the study shows that ACC neurons encode the value of both fixated and peripherally presented (even never-fixated) cues, guiding where subjects look next and what they ultimately choose, revealing parallel processing of fixated and unfixated information during decision-making.

2026-11Relevance 0.54doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-78945-4

AbstractAbstract We use our eyes to evaluate objects around us. Models of economic choice emphasise the impact of sequential fixation on valuation and choice, with prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) encoding values of fixated items. Yet whether these regions also evaluate non-fixated peripheral information to guide choice remains unknown. Here we show that in a decision-making task involving four simultaneously presented cues, rhesus macaques use peripheral information to guide information sampling and choice, and that ACC neurons encode this peripheral information. This served two distinct purposes: subjects were more likely to fixate valuable peripheral cues, and more likely to choose valuable options whose cues were never even fixated. ACC, orbitofrontal cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex neurons all encoded cue value as they were fixated. ACC was unique, however, in also conducting parallel processing of peripheral cues, including cues never fixated, to guide information sampling and choice.

13Consumer neuroscience: the emerging science of everyday choices

Plassmann, H., Karmarkar, U., Venkatraman, V.

This review paper surveys the field of consumer neuroscience, covering methods, formal models, and multidataset approaches that link neural and physiological processes to consumer choice and decision-making behavior. It highlights trends in behavioral prediction, brain-body integration, and formal computational modeling relevant to everyday decisions.

2026-11Relevance 0.51doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2026.10.005

AbstractConsumer neuroscience is a developing field at the intersection of neuroscience, psychology, economics, and marketing that seeks to uncover the neural and physiological mechanisms underlying consumer behavior. Building on early psychophysiological studies, the field has expanded substantially since the mid-2000s through an increasing range of methods and models. We highlight that its strength lies in spanning multiple levels of analysis to address marketing-relevant questions. We highlight four major trends driving the field: improving behavioral prediction, synthesizing evidence through multidataset analyses, developing formal models linking neural processes to consumer decision-making, and integrating brain and body measures. Together, these advances position consumer neuroscience as an integrative science with implications for marketing, policy, and consumer welfare.
